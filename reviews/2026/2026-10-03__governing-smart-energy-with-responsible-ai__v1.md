# Paper Review

## Review

Hlalat and co-authors examine AI-mediated decision-making in smart energy markets through comparative doctrinal legal analysis across the European Union, United Kingdom, United States, Australia, and Singapore. Their central move is to reject responsible AI as a standalone ethical or technical objective and recast it as a legally structured process. The proposed Digital Energy Justice Framework organizes that process around four linked dimensions: algorithmic procedural safeguards, institutional accountability, energy-consumer protection, and rights-based remedies. In governance terms, this is a useful reframing because it connects system design to the institutions that must explain, supervise, review, correct, and ultimately answer for decisions affecting access, pricing, profiling, demand management, and other functions tied to an essential service.

The paper establishes an important distinction between technical transparency and consumer-oriented explainability. Documentation about models, data, testing, and system design does not by itself give an affected consumer a usable account of what happened, who is responsible, or how to challenge an adverse result. The authors extend that distinction into a procedural chain linking transparency, explainability, contestability, human review, complaint, and redress. They also identify lifecycle accountability as necessary where responsibility is distributed among energy providers, technology vendors, data processors, regulators, and public authorities. That shifts the question from whether AI is explainable in the abstract to whether institutional responsibility remains traceable when multiple actors contribute to a decision.

The evidence base is doctrinal and comparative rather than empirical. Legal and regulatory materials are organized across eight common dimensions including transparency, responsibility, human oversight, non-discrimination, consumer protection, supervision, contestability, and remedies. Ofgem guidance and consumer research provide the most concrete sectoral evidence, while the wider jurisdictional comparison supplies differences in legal architecture and enforceability. This supports the framework as a normative-analytical structure, but not as proof that the proposed safeguards currently prevent harm or that complaint and remedy systems work effectively in practice. The authors state this limitation directly and call for socio-legal testing involving consumers, regulators, providers, and developers.

The principal governance gap therefore lies in execution. The framework specifies what responsible institutional behaviour should contain but does not define how conflicting responsibilities are resolved, which actor bears residual liability when accountability chains fail, what minimum evidentiary record must survive an automated decision, or how regulators should test whether nominal human oversight has real authority to intervene. Its emphasis on existing complaint and regulatory channels is plausible, especially because energy, consumer, equality, and data-protection law already provide remedial routes. The harder question is whether those institutions possess the capacity, technical access, and jurisdictional clarity needed to reconstruct AI-mediated decisions and compel correction. If those conditions are absent, transparency can become disclosure without contestability and human oversight can become procedural theatre.

The paper's durable contribution is therefore not the invention of new principles, which the authors explicitly disclaim, but the assembly of familiar AI-governance and energy-justice requirements into a sector-specific legal chain. That makes a practical institutional proposition visible: in essential-service domains, responsible AI cannot be assessed at the model boundary alone. It has to be evaluated across the full path from deployment authority to explanation, responsibility, consumer access, review, and remedy. What would materially strengthen the framework is an operational assurance layer that specifies evidence requirements, responsibility hand-offs, review authority, escalation paths, and tests for whether remedies are actually reachable by digitally excluded or otherwise vulnerable consumers.

## Key Insight

Responsible AI governance in essential services becomes meaningful only when transparency is connected to identifiable institutional responsibility, accessible contestation, and enforceable remedy.
