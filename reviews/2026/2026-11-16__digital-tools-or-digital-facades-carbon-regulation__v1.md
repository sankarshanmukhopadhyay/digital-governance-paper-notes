# Paper Review

## Review

Qinyu Yu evaluates AI-enabled carbon regulation as an institutional chain rather than treating digital adoption as proof of better enforcement. The paper distinguishes information integrity, measurement validity and enforcement closure: whether observations and revisions can be reconstructed, whether indicators represent the regulated outcome, and whether a flagged problem reaches an independently verifiable disposition. These dimensions identify different control points. A traceable enterprise submission may still use an invalid emissions proxy; a correctly identified anomaly may still lack investigation or durable correction. The contribution is a criterion for examining regulatory capacity, not a claim that AI has produced a measured improvement in compliance.

The method is structured documentary comparison with bounded process tracing across Zhejiang, Hebei and Inner Mongolia under a national reporting framework. The cases reveal different observable capacities: integrated indicators, recurring records and independent physical-testing facilities. The author expressly avoids ranking provinces or assigning changes in emissions or enforcement to AI. The national review totals and falling reported error rates establish administrative activity but do not identify which alerts caused investigations or corrections. The study carefully classifies the reported manipulation of coal testing records and dates as observed conduct, potential indicator substitution as an institutional vulnerability, and platform capture as unsupported. The investigated conduct predates the reviewed AI deployments and is not attributed to algorithmic detection.

The most consequential distinction is between a reporting system that improves the visibility of submissions and an evidentiary system capable of challenging the submissions themselves. The documented manipulation involved dates, report versions, sample identities and numerical parameters. It shows why preserving data inside an electronic platform is insufficient if reporting entities or contracted intermediaries can reconstruct the underlying evidence. Conversely, the paper's account of laboratory testing correctly treats independence as conditional on sample selection, custody and linkage to an administrative decision. Figure-free Table 1 makes the limits visible: every implementation setting lacks publicly accessible records joining detection, verified findings and final disposition.

A significant traceability defect qualifies the paper's empirical foundation. Sections 4.1 and 5 invoke numbered references [1] and [2] for specific national regulations and a 2023 government notice, while the bibliography lists two 2018 academic articles at those numbers. Reference [3], repeatedly used for a national ministry's 2025 platform statistics, is a 2023 machine-learning research article. References [11]–[14] likewise identify scholarly publications, not the particular official presentations, provincial responses, laboratory reports or ministry investigation bulletin described in the text. The claims may be based on genuine public records, but those records cannot be retrieved from the cited entries as supplied. This is not evidence that the institutional facts are false; it materially limits independent replication of the source chain on which the comparison relies.

The author's methodological restraint mitigates a second limitation: public disclosure is selective, so missing case-level closure records cannot be treated as failures to close cases. The proposed remedy is therefore appropriately evidentiary. Uniform provenance and revision histories, reviewer access to original inputs, independent checks on model flags and unflagged cases, and case-linked disclosure of corrective action would expose whether the full sequence operates. Yet the proposed public indicators require stable case identifiers and consistent definitions of confirmation, dismissal, correction and recurrence, with protected access to commercially sensitive records. Without these, reporting more counts can reproduce the measurement problem the paper diagnoses.

Compared with the archive's *Accountability in AI and Automated Decision-Making*, this analysis specifies how reviewability can be tested within a multilevel emissions regime, especially when enterprises and contractors supply the state's evidence. Its novelty is the three-part application and the refusal to infer a province-wide failure from a single incident, not the invention of independent audit or regulatory feedback. The next evidentiary step is to publish the missing primary-source references and reconstruct a sample of cases from raw observation through signed revisions, independent verification, decision, correction and follow-up. The paper establishes why that chain matters and which links remain publicly unverified; it does not demonstrate AI-specific causal efficacy.

## Key Insight

Digital regulatory credibility requires independent source evidence, valid measurements and documented enforcement closure; more extensive screening cannot substitute for any of these conditions.
