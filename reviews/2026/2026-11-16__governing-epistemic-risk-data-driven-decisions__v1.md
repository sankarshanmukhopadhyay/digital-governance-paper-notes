# Paper Review

## Review

Kilwake and Ongare propose the Epistemic Risk Governance Framework (ERGF) to examine how unreliable or unjustified knowledge arises across collection, integration, modelling, circulation and decision. They join data governance, platform power, algorithmic accountability and information integrity into a lifecycle assessment. The value of the integration is that an explainable decision can still rest on selective data or information whose visibility is controlled by an intermediary. Table 2 locates thirteen risks; Table 3 maps them onto six diagnostic dimensions. The treatment of platform control over ranking, access and verification identifies a source of institutional power beyond model-level bias.

The method is conceptual design supported by an integrative literature review, not an evaluated governance intervention. Section 2.3 describes coding sources by lifecycle stage, actor and response, then merging overlapping risks. However, Section 2.2 retains an editorial instruction to insert numbers of records identified, screened and retained. The concept matrix is available only on request. Without screening counts, exclusion decisions and coded evidence, the completeness of the taxonomy and dimensions cannot be reproduced. The funding statement also retains a template placeholder. These are defects in the published manuscript, not evidence that its conceptual distinctions are false.

Proposition P7 warrants particular scrutiny. It claims that upstream data and platform weaknesses constrain downstream transparency, information integrity and oversight, describing governance as non-compensatory. This is credible when reviewers inherit unrecoverable or untraceable evidence, but the paper does not specify which defects are irreversible. Independent sampling, external records or testimony may reveal and correct errors introduced upstream. Testing P7 requires differentiating an irretrievable loss from a defect later controls can recover, and specifying the predicted interaction rather than assuming a universal weakest link. Table 4 expresses seven proposed relationships without observed effect estimates or validated measurement instruments.

Table 5's comparison with NIST AI RMF, ISO/IEC 42001, the EU AI Act, UNESCO guidance and Kenyan instruments is expressly indicative. Its coverage symbols lack provision-level mappings and independently reproducible scoring rules. Assigning ERGF full coverage in all dimensions describes the authors' framework design, not verified conformance or improved decision outcomes. The table therefore cannot establish that other instruments lack relevant protections. Kenya's lending, identity, e-hailing and election examples are illustrative diagnostic applications, not empirical evidence that ERGF mitigates those risks.

The central governance boundary remains who has authority to act on findings. D3 asks who controls access and visibility, but the framework does not determine who can compel disclosure or override a platform operator. D5 proposes human review and appeal without specifying independent access to evidence, decision rights, time limits or enforceable remedies. D4 identifies concentrated fact-checking authority but does not specify how a verifier's judgments are challenged or corrected. Data provenance establishes the history of an assertion, not the legitimacy of its originating authority or of the institution that accepts it. These are implementation dependencies appropriate to identify in a conceptual framework, not evidence of failed deployment.

Where the archive's *Governance Laundering* review addresses whether operational control evidence survives independent reconstruction, ERGF asks which claims became visible and credible in the first place. Its defensible role is a research and audit-planning taxonomy. Evaluation should release the coding record, preregister observable definitions for P1–P7, compare assessor agreement, and trace contested decisions through evidence access, review and effective correction. That would test whether ERGF adds discrimination beyond existing assessments without equating a complete checklist with trustworthy governance.

## Key Insight

Governing the reliability of data-driven decisions requires scrutiny of who controls evidence and verification across the entire decision chain; provenance and procedural controls alone cannot establish legitimate authority.
