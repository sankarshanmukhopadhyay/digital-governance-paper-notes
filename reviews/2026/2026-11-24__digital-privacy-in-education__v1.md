# Paper Review

## Review

Matyas Koszegi's critical narrative review identifies educational procurement as a consequential point of control over student privacy. Sections 3 and 7 connect mandatory platform participation to the limitations of consent: students who cannot realistically refuse a learning or examination system cannot reliably constrain its collection practices through individual choice. The institutional decision to adopt a platform therefore determines a substantial portion of their privacy exposure. The paper's contribution is to connect this asymmetry to the distribution of implementation responsibilities in Japan rather than to present privacy as a matter of individual literacy or technical security alone.

The Japanese case is instructive because national digitalization and decentralized implementation coexist. Section 5 describes the GIGA School Program, the amended Act on the Protection of Personal Information, the Personal Information Protection Commission and MEXT's April 2026 educational-data guidance alongside local procurement and configuration responsibilities. The author distinguishes the existence of national rules from the capacity of schools and municipalities to give them effect. This also avoids attributing institutional outcomes to unsubstantiated generalizations about Japanese attitudes to privacy.

The evidentiary basis is appropriately bounded. Section 2 describes a purposive narrative synthesis of scholarship, official sources and documented case evidence, not a systematic review or original empirical study. Section 5.4 cites Human Rights Watch's 2022 observations that nine of ten examined MEXT-endorsed products surveilled or could surveil children and seven transmitted children's data to advertising technology companies. The author explicitly rejects a population-wide prevalence inference. The observations establish that endorsement and educational purpose cannot guarantee privacy-preserving implementation; they do not establish the incidence of such practices after the subsequent governance reforms or the effectiveness of particular local procurement arrangements.

The institutional consequence is that procurement must govern more than the decision to acquire a platform. Section 7 proposes contractual specifications for processing purposes, subprocessors, retention and deletion, together with impact assessment, data-flow mapping and configuration controls. These are appropriate intervention points. However, the initial procurement decision cannot establish that subsequent processing remains necessary or proportionate. Changes in telemetry, subprocessors or analytical capabilities can alter the privacy exposure of students without changing their obligation to participate. This is a reviewer inference from the paper's treatment of function creep and local implementation, not a demonstrated pattern of post-procurement change in Japanese schools.

A reasonable counterargument is that the author already treats governance as an ongoing responsibility, recommends shared technical expertise and calls for research into actual institutional practice. The absence of a tested procurement regime is therefore an evidence boundary rather than a defect in the narrative method. The unresolved question is whether local authorities can inspect changes, require correction, suspend unjustified processing and provide meaningful alternatives without interrupting educational access. Decentralization may preserve local discretion while making privacy outcomes dependent on uneven administrative resources. Shared assessment and configuration services could reduce this disparity, but would themselves require transparent governance and locally usable evidence.

The qualified treatment of free and open-source software in section 7.5 reinforces this judgment. Code availability can support inspection, portability and institutional hosting, but does not establish the privacy properties of a running service. Local hosting may transfer maintenance and security burdens to institutions without the resources to discharge them. The relevant comparison is therefore between actual governance and deployment arrangements, not software licensing categories alone.

The archive's reviews of *The Right to Privacy in Tanzania in the Era of Digital Surveillance* and *Who Authorises Surveillance?* distinguish formal protections from effective constraints on surveillance authority. Koszegi extends that concern into education, where the procurement relationship can determine the practical choices of learners who cannot readily withdraw. The paper supplies a useful synthesis rather than a novel enforcement mechanism. Its recommendations should be adopted as assessment criteria, not treated as demonstrated privacy outcomes. Comparative examination of network flows, contract enforcement, retention and deletion evidence, and responses to contested processing across municipalities would establish whether those criteria produce sustained protection without making access to education conditional on unnecessary disclosure.

## Key Insight

Where students cannot meaningfully refuse an educational platform, procurement becomes a collective privacy decision whose legitimacy depends on institutions retaining the capacity to constrain data use throughout the deployment lifecycle.
