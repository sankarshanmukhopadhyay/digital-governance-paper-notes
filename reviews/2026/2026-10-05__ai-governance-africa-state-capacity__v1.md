# Paper Review

## Review

“Artificial Intelligence Governance in Africa” argues that the central problem facing African AI governance is shifting from policy absence toward implementation capacity. Drawing on the African Union’s Continental AI Strategy, OECD assessments, UNESCO readiness work, World Bank analysis, and Nigeria as an illustrative national case, the paper distinguishes policy formulation from the institutional machinery required to make policy operative. Its core proposition is straightforward but consequential: a government may possess formal regulatory authority while lacking the personnel, technical expertise, coordination mechanisms, infrastructure, financing, data systems, monitoring tools, or enforcement capacity needed to exercise that authority effectively.

The paper’s strongest contribution is this separation of authority from capability. It treats AI governance as a state-capacity problem rather than a race to enact dedicated AI legislation. Existing data-protection, telecommunications, financial, consumer-protection, cybersecurity, and competition institutions may already hold relevant mandates. The practical question is whether those institutions can interpret AI systems, obtain credible evidence, coordinate across overlapping jurisdictions, assess compliance, and enforce decisions. This framing usefully shifts attention from visible policy outputs such as strategies, councils, or new agencies toward the less visible administrative infrastructure through which governance actually occurs.

The systems perspective is also valuable. The paper connects institutional capacity to compute, connectivity, data quality, technical skills, financing, procurement, public administration, and regional cooperation. These are not independent policy variables. Infrastructure constrains regulatory capability; staffing affects enforcement; fragmented mandates weaken coordination; external cloud and technology dependencies can narrow practical sovereignty; and weak administrative systems can cause AI to automate existing institutional deficiencies rather than correct them. The discussion of government as both regulator and user of AI further shows why internal public-sector controls, procurement standards, auditability, human oversight, and review mechanisms belong inside the governance architecture.

The evidentiary limits are equally important. The paper is a policy synthesis rather than a comparative empirical study of African regulatory performance. Its central claim that implementation capacity has become the principal governance constraint is supported largely by continental and multilateral assessments, not by a common country-level measurement framework across jurisdictions. Nigeria is explicitly presented as illustrative rather than representative. The paper therefore establishes a persuasive implementation hypothesis and a useful analytical framework, but not a measured ranking of which capacity deficits are most binding, where, or with what causal effect on governance outcomes.

A further gap lies between identifying capacity inputs and specifying institutional performance. Training officials, establishing coordination bodies, increasing compute access, or creating monitoring indicators are necessary activities, but they do not by themselves establish accountable governance. The paper begins to address this by distinguishing activities, outputs, intermediate outcomes, and broader governance outcomes. That distinction should be pushed further into operational metrics for regulatory response time, audit coverage, complaint resolution, enforcement consistency, procurement compliance, contestability, and the quality of cross-agency decisions.

The durable governance judgment is that AI policy cannot be evaluated by the existence of strategies or laws alone. Effective governance requires administrative institutions capable of converting legal authority into repeatable, evidence-based action. For African states, the most productive policy question is therefore not simply whether to regulate AI, but which institutional functions must become reliable enough for regulation to have practical force.

## Key Insight

AI governance becomes effective only when formal policy authority is converted into funded mandates, technical capability, coordination, monitoring, enforcement, and institutions able to sustain those functions over time.
