# Paper Review

## Review

Caroline Cauffman examines a consequence of recognizing consumer delegation rights that is easily missed when regulation is framed as enabling access. A positive right to use a consumer AI agent (CAIA) can communicate institutional confidence in the activity while accelerating investment in the infrastructure through which consumers exercise that right. The article distinguishes this expressive and market-constituting effect from a narrower prohibition on unjustified obstruction. Its recommendation is to establish consumer protection and market contestability early, but defer general positive recognition until delegated actions can be performed with acceptable reliability and the legal framework is practically enforceable.

Section II reconstructs the European Law Institute's Guiding Principles and Model Rules on Digital Assistants for Consumer Contracts (DACC). Parameter selection, confirmation or objection, deactivation, decision documentation, conflict disclosure, attribution exceptions and supplier liability establish grounds for trust in specified safeguards. Section III identifies the regulatory safety halo that arises when consumers extend this justified confidence to the agent's substantive competence. Compliance does not establish reliable interpretation of incomplete preferences, resistance to manipulation or suitability of a purchase. The distinction is especially important where harmonised standards establish a presumption of conformity limited to particular legal requirements.

The evidence supports the risk mechanism, not its magnitude. The cited shopping benchmarks demonstrate limitations under controlled conditions; task completion is not a measure of actual consumer transaction error rates. Studies of compliance costs, network effects and SME digitalization support plausible concentration mechanisms without measuring the effect of a DACC-style right. The article is a doctrinal and political-economic argument rather than a causal evaluation of enacted legislation. Its caution about generalizing those findings is justified, but the same evidentiary boundary applies to its preferred regulatory sequence.

The treatment of redress makes the institutional problem concrete. Under the proposed attribution regime, an unwanted transaction need not constitute non-conformity, trader manipulation or a departure from reasonable expectations. Even where a remedy exists, consumers may have to detect the problem, identify the responsible party and obtain technically difficult evidence held by suppliers, traders and other services. Confirmation does not reverse disclosures during earlier stages of a transaction. Cauffman's proposals for collective and public enforcement accordingly address a real gap between legal entitlement and accessible remedy without claiming that compensation can undo every consequence.

Section IV supplies the distinctive contribution. Early agents backed by established operating systems, browsers, marketplaces and payment integrations can gain adoption and bargaining power before independent providers construct comparable relationships. Fixed compliance costs can reinforce this advantage. Article 5(4) DACC provides access to parameter records but does not establish functional portability of the agent relationship; exporting data does not ensure that a competing service can reproduce the relevant constraints, preferences or account arrangements. Similarly, exemptions from machine-readability duties can reduce SME compliance burdens without making their offers legible to agents. Intermediaries that translate those offers can acquire control over the terms of commercial visibility.

A further consequence is that freedom to delegate may weaken freedom not to delegate. Section IV.5 explains that formal access through human-facing channels may survive while prices, availability or service quality deteriorate relative to agent-mediated channels. This makes the quality of alternative participation a substantive governance question rather than a matter of interface availability.

The unresolved issue is whether postponing general positive recognition is the best response. Acceptable reliability depends on the action, the discretion granted, the evidence available and the reversibility of consequences. A broad maturity threshold could obscure differences between narrow purchasing mandates and open-ended transactional discretion. Delay may also preserve incumbent control over independent agents' access. A fair counterargument is that the author expressly supports early intervention and distinguishes anti-obstruction duties from positive recognition. That distinction addresses the objection to regulatory abstention, but does not establish whether differentiated delegation entitlements with bounded assurance would produce better outcomes than deferral. This remains a comparative policy question.

The archive's *The Agentic Web Requires New Normative Infrastructure* treats platform restrictions on delegated access as a governance object; *Designing Loyalty: AI Agents and Conflicts of Interest* examines the duties arising within the agent relationship. Cauffman adds the regulatory timing question: recognizing an entitlement can alter the distribution of power among the intermediaries needed to exercise it. Her insistence that legislators determine essential rights and risks rather than leaving them to technical standardization follows directly from that concern.

The article establishes a persuasive case for separating legal recognition, scoped assurance and effective remedy, and for addressing market contestability before integration patterns become entrenched. It does not establish a general readiness threshold or demonstrate that delaying positive recognition dominates a differentiated approach. The appropriate policy assessment should compare bounded entitlements and narrower access protections against evidence of independent-agent entry, functional switching, affordable redress, SME discoverability and non-agent service quality. The right to delegate should be evaluated by the distribution of practical choices it preserves, not merely by the transactions it enables.

## Key Insight

A right to delegate consumer decisions to AI can strengthen the intermediaries through which that right must be exercised unless assurance remains task-bounded, agent relationships are portable, and participation without delegation remains materially viable.
