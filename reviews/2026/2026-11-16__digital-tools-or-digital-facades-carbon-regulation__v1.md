# Paper Review

## Review

Qinyu Yu evaluates AI-enabled carbon regulation as an institutional chain rather than treating digital adoption as evidence of effective enforcement. The paper separates information integrity, measurement validity and enforcement closure: whether inputs and revisions are reconstructable, whether indicators represent the regulated outcome, and whether a flagged problem reaches verified disposition. These are independent conditions. A traceable submission may use an invalid emissions proxy; a valid anomaly may never result in correction.

The method is structured documentary comparison with bounded process tracing across Zhejiang, Hebei and Inner Mongolia. The cases expose different observable capacities: integrated indicators, frequent records and physical-testing facilities. The author rejects provincial rankings and avoids attributing emissions or enforcement outcomes to AI. National review totals and falling error rates establish administrative activity, not which alerts caused investigations or corrections. The paper differentiates reported alteration of coal-testing records and dates as documented conduct, indicator substitution as a possible vulnerability, and platform capture as unsubstantiated.

The central institutional problem is whether public authorities can challenge evidence supplied by enterprises and contracted intermediaries. The documented manipulation concerned sample identities, dates, report versions and numerical inputs. Preserving submitted data in a platform would not itself establish the authenticity of those inputs. Independent laboratory testing can provide a separate evidentiary basis, but its value depends on sampling, custody and connection to subsequent decisions. Table 1 shows that none of the reviewed public records supplies the full case-level chain from anomaly through investigation and final disposition. Absence of such public evidence is not evidence that agencies failed to resolve cases.

One material defect limits the article's source traceability. Sections 4.1 and 5 cite [1] and [2] for national regulations and a 2023 ministry notice, but the bibliography identifies two academic articles from 2018. Reference [3], used for a ministry's 2025 national platform figures, identifies a 2023 machine-learning study. References [11]–[14] also list scholarly publications rather than the particular government presentations, provincial replies, laboratory reports and official investigation bulletin described in the narrative. These mismatches constrain independent replication of the documentary comparison; they do not independently disprove the reported events.

The paper proposes source-linked version histories, access to raw evidence, independent review of flagged and unflagged cases, and public disclosure connecting alerts with correction and closure. These recommendations respond to the stated evidence gap, although they remain proposals rather than tested reforms. A workable reporting standard would need stable case identifiers, definitions of confirmed and dismissed alerts, identified responsibility for follow-up, and a way to verify persistence of correction without publishing commercially sensitive records. Otherwise improved publication of aggregate counts would leave the paper's principal question unanswered.

Compared with this archive's *Accountability in AI and Automated Decision-Making*, the article provides a domain-specific test of administrative reviewability where regulated parties also supply compliance evidence. The contribution is a three-dimensional framework that separates observed conduct from institutional vulnerabilities. A stronger assessment requires corrected primary-source citations and a reproducible sample of cases linking raw readings, revisions, independent verification, administrative decision and verified remediation. The present evidence supports the institutional threshold for credible enforcement, not AI-specific causal efficacy or a provincial performance ranking.

## Key Insight

Digital regulatory credibility requires independent source evidence, valid measurements and documented enforcement closure; more extensive screening cannot substitute for any of these conditions.
