# Paper Review

## Review

Johannes Johnen and Shiva Shekhar distinguish product-specific network effects, which reinforce a downstream rival's own product and increase an integrated platform's incentive to foreclose it, from ecosystem-specific effects, through which the rival's activity improves shared infrastructure and can make accommodation more profitable. The proposed regulatory screen asks which effect dominates in the relevant market segment, not merely whether network effects exist. The authors recommend stricter scrutiny without presumed efficiencies when product-specific effects dominate and conventional vertical-merger analysis when ecosystem-specific effects are affirmatively supported.

The causal mechanism is intelligible: exclusion yields diversion to the integrated firm's downstream offering but also forfeits whatever shared ecosystem value the excluded rival generates. The authors identify a threshold, expressed using α in their underlying model, at which the latter loss can outweigh diversion gains. Their paper is a nine-page condensed account of a longer formal article, not a presentation of the full model equations or an empirical estimation strategy. Consequently, the qualitative ordering of incentives is explained, but the reader cannot reproduce threshold estimates or assess their sensitivity from this text alone. Microsoft/Activision illustrates the need to distinguish console and cloud-streaming segments; Booking/eTraveli illustrates why reinforcing an ecosystem can raise entry barriers even where excluding complementors is unattractive. These are interpretive case applications, not tests of the screen's predictive performance.

The proposal is deliberately limited to first-pass classification. This matters when assessing its principal vulnerability: a platform may design data flows, interoperability and shared services to generate ecosystem spillovers and then cite those spillovers as grounds for lighter review. The authors recognize that network-effect type is partly endogenous and place the evidentiary burden on the firm asserting ecosystem-wide efficiencies. Their proposed checks include demand interdependence, shared-service data flows, improvements attributable to third-party participation, and conduct concerning interoperability and nondiscrimination. They also advise treating mixed preliminary evidence as product-specific. These safeguards make the screen more defensible than a categorical presumption that ecosystem effects are benign.

The remaining institutional issue concerns what the screening outcome authorizes the regulator to infer. A lower incentive to exclude a current complementor is not evidence of low barriers to entry into the ecosystem, independent access to the shared layer, or constraints on the operator's future ranking, data and interface decisions. The authors explicitly recognize this in their Booking/eTraveli discussion, which separates foreclosure from entrenchment. A consistent application must therefore preserve parallel theories of harm instead of treating the ecosystem-specific classification as a general clearance signal. The proposed accommodation threshold also requires evidence about the marginal value rivals add to shared services, not merely proof that common APIs or data pipelines exist.

For reproducible administrative use, authorities would need to record the market-segment boundary, observed rival-to-ecosystem spillovers, counterevidence from discriminatory conduct, the treatment of uncertain or mixed effects, and the specific foreclosure theory being screened. An adverse or favorable classification should remain open to challenge using the same evidence record. Evidence that would justify greater confidence includes independently testable estimates of marginal ecosystem contribution and cases where the screen's predictions can be compared with subsequent access, steering and entry outcomes. The contribution is a useful separation of incentive mechanisms, not a demonstrated method for establishing overall market contestability.

## Key Insight

A platform's incentive to accommodate complementary rivals does not establish that its ecosystem is contestable or resistant to entrenchment.
