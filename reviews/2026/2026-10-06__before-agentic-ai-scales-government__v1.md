# Paper Review

## Review

Salah and colleagues identify a prospective democratic authorization gap: public institutions may be unable to demonstrate that actions an agent selects, sequences or executes remain connected to authority they were entitled to exercise and delegate. Answerability after an outcome and compliance with configured permissions do not establish that antecedent entitlement. The paper therefore treats procurement and piloting as occasions to define the permitted action space before contracts, interfaces and routines make the arrangement difficult to change.

This is an anticipatory Perspective grounded in political delegation, administrative law and agent scholarship, not an empirical investigation of authorization failures. Section 3 distinguishes mandate translation, recursive delegation, action diffusion and contestability lag. Those mechanisms connect the problem to concrete administrative consequences: choices about records, priorities and notices can redistribute burdens while still serving a broad objective. The authors expressly avoid treating every tool invocation as legal delegation; the relevant question is whether a component exercises consequential discretion or performs an act attributable to public authority.

Section 4 supplies five pilot conditions: bounded authorization, permission inheritance, action-level traceability, named institutional responsibility, and interruption with reversible redress. These move beyond nominal human oversight. An institution must identify who approves changes, suspends operation and reviews contested cases, while vendor contracts cannot displace public responsibility. The remedy extends to correcting records, cancelling pending steps and restoring an administrative position where feasible. That qualification matters: the authors do not assume that every effect can be reversed.

The conceptual boundary needs further specification. Inability to demonstrate authority, actual absence of lawful power and inadequate democratic justification are related but distinct failures. Missing records can obstruct proof of a lawful act; a perfectly recorded act can still exceed institutional competence. Likewise, a general mandate may lawfully permit bounded discretion without prescribing every operational choice. The paper's limitation is not that it permits such discretion, but that it provides no worked case distinguishing lawful translation, evidentiary failure and substantively unauthorized expansion. Its chain metaphor also needs to accommodate overlapping legal powers and independent review rather than imply that one unbroken lineage settles legitimacy.

The strongest response is that a short Perspective proposes conditions for experimentation, not a jurisdiction-independent legal decision procedure. That resolves demands for a universal implementation. It does not resolve the pilot's immediate institutional choices: who interprets disputed powers, which changes require reauthorization, how suspension reaches queued or delegated actions, and who can require a remedy. Action-level logging supports those judgments but does not deliver them. My further inference is that detailed traces become a new administrative gatekeeping surface if vendors or agencies control access to the evidence citizens need to challenge a decision.

Alongside the archive's Authorization Architectures for Tool-Using AI Agents, this article supplies the upstream question that enforcement cannot answer alone: was the institution entitled to grant the scope being enforced? Compared with Procedural fairness, accountability, and legitimacy in the AI-enabled state, it concerns justified authority rather than perceived legitimacy. Its contribution is therefore a pilot-governance admission principle, not proof that the proposed conditions prevent harm.

My judgment is that scaling should depend on demonstrated authority and workable challenge procedures alongside technical performance. A pilot should map one consequential workflow from legal basis through reserved decisions, permission changes, execution evidence and remedy. Tests should include a lawful but poorly documented action, a logged action exceeding authority, cancellation during sub-delegation and a citizen challenge before an administrative deadline. Independent review, measured interruption times, evidence access and completed remedies would show whether the five conditions constrain public power rather than merely document its exercise.

## Key Insight

An auditable permission chain can show how an agent acted without establishing that the public institution was entitled to authorize it or that a citizen can obtain timely redress.
