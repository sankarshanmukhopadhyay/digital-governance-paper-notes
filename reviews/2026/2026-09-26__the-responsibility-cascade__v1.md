# Paper Review

## Review

Kalluri argues that agentic AI changes responsibility from a point attribution problem into a temporal governance problem. In multi-step workflows, initiators, checkpoint approvers, designers, deployers, and organizations occupy different positions of knowledge and control, while AI mediates decisions without itself bearing moral responsibility. The Sequential Responsibility Attribution Framework (SRAF) separates initiation, checkpoint, delegation, and outcome responsibility, then diagnoses dilution, checkpoint accumulation, crumple cascades, and delegation vacuums.

The governance contribution is to relocate accountability from nominal human presence to the structure of decision rights across a workflow. SRAF asks who could act, what they knew, and how much control they possessed at each decision point. Its design implications are concrete: checkpoints should scale with stakes and irreversibility, intervention should remain genuinely reversible, logs should preserve responsibility-relevant metadata, and high-stakes deployments should undergo responsibility failure-mode analysis. Meaningful human control is therefore treated as a distributed institutional property rather than a single approval event.

The framework remains more diagnostic than operational. Its proposed allocation of outcome responsibility combines causal contribution, knowledge, and control, but supplies no reproducible method for measuring or aggregating those factors. The three cases illustrate the taxonomy rather than validate it, and the approach assumes sufficiently complete workflow evidence to reconstruct responsibility after failure. It also leaves unresolved who adjudicates disputed assignments, how blame-shifting checkpoints are detected, and what enforcement or redress follows when an organization creates a foreseeable delegation vacuum.

Relative to adjacent archive work on humans being pushed out of the loop and on named decision ownership, this paper adds a temporal model of how responsibility changes as delegated work unfolds. Accountability cannot be attached after failure to the nearest human actor. Governance must preserve epistemic access, intervention authority, traceable delegation, and organizational responsibility where consequential pathways are created.
