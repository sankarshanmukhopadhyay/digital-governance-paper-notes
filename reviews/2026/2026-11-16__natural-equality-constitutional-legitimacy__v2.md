# Paper Review

## Review

Simon uses Locke's account of natural equality and political power held in fiduciary trust to examine algorithmically mediated public authority. The contribution is normative rather than technical: an automated decision does not become politically legitimate because it is accurate, consistently applied or legally compliant at the level of a software procedure. Legitimacy requires public reasons, an identifiable authority answerable to those affected, and a continuing ability to contest the decision. These are conditions governing the institution's exercise of power, not attributes conferred by a model's provenance or performance.

The reconstruction is careful about its intended scope. Sections 2–3 connect equal moral standing, reason and conditional consent to the idea that political judgment is never finally surrendered to the ruler. Section 5 derives intelligibility, fiduciary accountability and contestability as a test for computational governance. The author explicitly distinguishes an individual administrative appeal from Locke's extraordinary collective withdrawal of political trust. That matters: the ability to correct an individual decision cannot replace democratic authorization, while democratic authorization does not eliminate obligations to particular persons when administration goes wrong.

The interpretation earns an affirmative conclusion about one proposition. A state body cannot transfer its fiduciary duties to a vendor, data provider or technical decision aid. Where a public institution selects a system's objectives, data sources, thresholds or role in administration, those choices remain exercises of public power. The paper also acknowledges that participatory mechanisms can reproduce power asymmetries: expert audit assemblies may concentrate epistemic control, data trusts may blur responsibility, and regulatory sandboxes may advantage better-resourced actors. Its criterion therefore does not treat participation as a self-sufficient badge of legitimacy.

The strongest unresolved conceptual boundary is intelligibility. The text sometimes suggests that opaque computational operations make legitimate authority impossible, yet Section 4.1 recognizes that algorithmic systems can restrain arbitrary human discretion and improve consistency. These positions are compatible only if intelligibility means that the institution can justify the purpose, criteria, relevant evidence and effects of a decision to an affected person, rather than that a citizen must understand the technical internals of every model. The paper rejects source-code publication as a sufficient response, but does not fully delimit when a decision is intelligible despite technical opacity. A strict requirement of model-level interpretability would exclude forms of independently reviewable administrative assistance that the article otherwise permits.

That distinction should govern how the criterion is used. An unexplained model score adopted mechanically by an official fails the fiduciary test even when predictive performance is excellent. A technically opaque model may be compatible with the test if the state can independently establish the decision's evidentiary basis, disclose governing reasons, reject unsupported outputs and provide binding reconsideration. These are possible institutional configurations, not empirical cases that Simon has demonstrated. They show why the decisive authority question cannot be answered by inspecting model transparency alone.

The next boundary is enforcement capacity. Identifying an accountable public official does not establish that the institution can obtain records from contractors, inspect disparate impacts or order effective correction. The paper addresses the legitimacy of such power, not the procurement rights and remedial machinery needed to exercise it. That is a reasonable limit of philosophical reconstruction, but the criterion should not be treated as an implemented audit profile.

The archive's review of *Procedural Fairness, Accountability, and Legitimacy in the AI-Enabled State* concerns how legitimacy is perceived. *Accountability in AI and Automated Decision-Making* examines institutional review powers in administration. Simon contributes the normative reason why even popular, fair-seeming or efficient automated administration must remain subject to public justification. My conclusion is therefore affirmative but bounded: her fiduciary criterion supplies a clear reason to reject self-justifying automated state power, while the distinction between intelligible reasons and intelligible model operations must be settled before the criterion can decide contested deployment cases.

## Key Insight

Simon's Lockean criterion disallows treating accurate or consistently applied automated decisions as self-justifying public authority; the decisive question is whether an accountable institution can supply reasons and exercise corrective power, not whether every model operation is explainable.
