# Paper Review

## Review

Nedim Serhat Bilecen examines the gap between the ethical language surrounding artificial intelligence in journalism and the conditions under which AI is actually introduced into newsroom production. Using qualitative document analysis, the paper compares the BBC's machine-learning principles, JournalismAI reports, Council of Europe standards, and supporting literature against recurring concerns of transparency, accountability, bias, diversity, and editorial autonomy. Its central claim is that newsroom AI guidelines often remain normatively ambitious but operationally weak because proprietary systems are opaque, news organizations depend on major technology providers, and commercial incentives such as engagement, search optimization, and cost reduction can override professional commitments.

The paper's most useful contribution is to relocate AI ethics from the level of declared principles to the institutional conditions required to make those principles effective. It identifies a real governance problem: a newsroom can promise human oversight, explainability, pluralism, or responsible use while lacking access to model behavior, training provenance, ranking logic, vendor controls, or internal capacity to test whether those commitments are being met. The analysis of recommender systems sharpens this tension because editorial pluralism and engagement optimization can encode competing objectives into the same infrastructure. In that setting, transparency is not only a disclosure problem. It is a question of who controls the system, which evidence is available for scrutiny, and which institution has authority to intervene when editorial values and optimization objectives conflict.

The methodological design is appropriate for examining how governance frameworks are written and where their internal assumptions break down. The paper makes its sampling criteria explicit and links its research questions to thematic categories and operational coding. However, the second research question reaches beyond what document analysis alone can establish. The article asks how theoretical principles diverge from actual newsroom implementation, yet it does not conduct interviews, ethnography, system audits, or direct observation of newsroom workflows. Its concrete examples, including Sports Illustrated and proprietary recommendation systems, illustrate plausible and important failure modes, but they do not establish the prevalence of those failures across the sector.

That evidentiary boundary matters for the paper's strongest formulation. The claim that algorithmic transparency is an institutional illusion, or that guidelines operate as ethics-washing or symbolic defense mechanisms, is more expansive than the evidence directly supports. The study establishes that important governance commitments can become non-operational under opacity, infrastructure dependency, weak sanctions, and commercial pressure. It does not demonstrate that institutions generally adopt those commitments primarily to manufacture legitimacy or shield themselves from scrutiny. That stronger proposition would require evidence about organizational intent, implementation behavior, compliance outcomes, and institutional response to detected failures.

The recommendations move in the right direction by calling for algorithmic audits, AI literacy, internal ethics committees, and stronger press-council authority. Their next step should be to specify governance mechanics. An effective regime would need defined audit rights over vendors and systems, decision owners for deployment and escalation, evidence-retention requirements, measurable pluralism and verification controls, incident reporting, procurement obligations, independent review, and consequences for non-compliance. Full technical transparency may remain impossible or undesirable in proprietary systems, but accountable governance does not require total visibility. It requires enough observable evidence, authority, and enforcement capacity to test whether editorial commitments constrain actual system behavior.

The durable governance lesson is therefore broader than journalism. Principles do not govern merely because institutions publish them. They govern only when linked to decision rights, operational controls, evidence, oversight, and sanctions capable of surviving commercial and infrastructural pressure.

## Key Insight

AI governance in journalism fails when ethical principles are published without enforceable decision rights, audit access, institutional capacity, and controls over dependence on proprietary infrastructure.
