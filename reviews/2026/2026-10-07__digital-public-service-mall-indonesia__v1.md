# Paper Review

## Review

Suwitri, Chen, Pradana, Perkasa and Bellastuti examine Indonesia’s Digital Public Service Mall (MPP Digital) as a centrally coordinated service platform implemented across a decentralized system. Their argument is institutional: the Ministry of Administrative and Bureaucratic Reform (KemenPANRB) supplies policy direction, standards and inter-agency coordination that help connect national service ambitions to local implementation. The case describes expansion from 21 regions at the June 2023 soft launch to 199 districts and cities by September 2024, alongside growth in selected service transactions through May 2025. The authors also identify uneven regional readiness, limited ICT staffing and infrastructure, bureaucratic resistance, coordination gaps and citizens’ digital-literacy barriers.

The paper makes a useful move by treating integration as an administrative and political coordination problem, not simply a software deployment. A common platform can reduce the burden of navigating multiple agencies and can give a central ministry practical influence over service standards and integration priorities. In a decentralized state, this coordinating capacity may make fragmented services more coherent. The case therefore helps show how digital public infrastructure can redistribute decision rights: authority over the conditions of connection and the shape of the shared service journey becomes more consequential at the coordinating centre.

The evidence supports a bounded account of implementation and uptake, but not the broader claims of improved service quality, satisfaction, trust or equity. The study describes document analysis, in-depth interviews, utilization records and thematic analysis, yet does not report interview numbers, selection, roles, fieldwork procedures or a sufficiently transparent coding trail. The quantitative indicators are largely changes in transaction volumes over a short period, without a counterfactual, clear denominators, comparable service baselines or disaggregated outcomes. Growth in use is evidence of use; it is not, by itself, evidence that services became faster, more accurate, fairer or more satisfactory. The paper sometimes treats usage figures as indicators of satisfaction and trust, but those constructs require direct measurement.

That limitation qualifies, rather than erases, the central-coordination argument. A reasonable counterargument is that this is an exploratory case study intended to expose implementation dynamics, not estimate causal effects. On that reading, the descriptive evidence is appropriate for showing how national coordination and local capacity interact. But the article also makes evaluative claims about efficiency, public response and the model’s suitability for replication. Those claims need evidence beyond adoption counts. The paper’s own account of 315 of 514 local governments not yet implementing MPP Digital by May 2025 is a material reminder that platform reach and equal service capability are different outcomes.

The governance question is not only whether services can be integrated, but who defines the standards, determines which agencies and services qualify, controls changes to the platform, and is answerable when integration fails or a person cannot use the digital route. The article recognizes regional capacity and literacy constraints and calls for grievance mechanisms, but does not examine their operation, the continued availability of assisted or offline access, appeal rights, service-level accountability, data-sharing safeguards or how citizens can correct errors across institutional boundaries. These are not proven defects in MPP Digital; they are consequential questions left outside the study’s evidence. Central coordination can reduce fragmentation while also concentrating gatekeeping power and creating shared dependencies. Interoperability establishes that systems can exchange information, not that the resulting rules or authority are legitimate.

The article’s contribution is a grounded account of central coordination as an implementation capability in a decentralized public-service system. Its evidence establishes expansion and rising use in selected services, and identifies institutional and regional constraints. It does not establish that the platform caused better outcomes or that a common digital channel has become inclusive. The next evidentiary step is a comparative, disaggregated evaluation of completion, time, error and appeal outcomes, including people who do not or cannot use the application, alongside explicit tracing of authority, accountability and assisted-service arrangements. Until then, MPP Digital is best understood as a promising coordination infrastructure whose public value depends on the institutions and remedies built around it.

## Key Insight

A shared service platform can make central coordination operational across decentralized government, but rising usage and formal integration do not establish equitable access, improved outcomes, or legitimate authority over the rules citizens must navigate.
