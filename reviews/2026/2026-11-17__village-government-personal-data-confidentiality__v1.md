# Paper Review

## Review

Rahmat Pirdaus, Yandi and Enny Agustina examine a specific conflict at the boundary between local administrative autonomy and national privacy law in Indonesia. Their argument is that the authority recognized for village governments through rights of origin (*hak asal-usul*) and village-scale local functions under Law No. 6 of 2014, as amended by Law No. 3 of 2024, does not displace the obligations of Law No. 27 of 2022 on Personal Data Protection (PDP Law). A village may have the governmental competence to collect population or welfare information without thereby possessing unrestricted authority to retain, repurpose, publish or share it. The paper characterizes this relation as *conditional authority*: the legality of exercising an existing administrative power depends on compliance with substantive data-protection constraints.

This is a useful administrative-law distinction because digital service delivery routinely bundles together decisions that require separate justification. The authority to issue a domicile certificate may justify collecting particular identifying facts; it does not automatically authorize an economic profile of the applicant or subsequent sharing for another programme. The article develops this point through purpose limitation, data minimization, storage limitation, accuracy, confidentiality and accountability (Results and Discussion, section 1, pp. 5–8). It also recognizes that data subjects have rights of information, access, correction and other forms of control that village administrations cannot simply treat as optional service features. A legal basis grounded in a public task is not a general license for information reuse.

The method is principally doctrinal. The Methods section (p. 4) describes statutory, conceptual and case approaches, with grammatical, systematic and teleological interpretation of relevant law. The paper develops a coherent normative reading of village authority and national privacy obligations, but does not supply a defined sample of villages, an original dataset, case-selection rules, or an inspectable series of adjudicated disputes. Its discussion of widespread unsafe storage, publicly disclosed records, uneven official capacity and limited local oversight draws on secondary material rather than a demonstrated nationwide assessment. The asserted scale of digital village-system deployment and the extent of particular violations should therefore be read as contextual claims, not findings verified by this study. The article establishes an interpretive proposition; it does not measure compliance rates, causal effects or the effectiveness of any proposed control.

The institutional account extends beyond confidentiality in the narrow technical sense. Section 2 (pp. 8–11) identifies potentially consequential relationships among local officials, higher-level administrative systems, national supervision, vendors and residents. The paper notes that systems at different levels of government often lack compatible formats and access arrangements, and that inter-agency exchange can occur without a clearly articulated lawful purpose or allocation of liability. Its discussion of social hierarchy is especially relevant: where citizens depend on village officials for certificates and benefits, a request for consent may be experienced as an instruction. This makes a formal consent record a poor substitute for establishing whether a public-service processing operation is required by law, optional, proportionate and genuinely contestable.

The decisive gap lies between identifying a village as a personal-data controller and establishing which institution actually determines each processing purpose and controls the means of execution. The paper invokes the statutory controller definition and assigns responsibility to village administrations (pp. 8–9), while acknowledging integrations with regional and national systems (p. 10). But it does not differentiate processing decided locally from processing prescribed by another authority, jointly determined, or technically operated by a contractor. A village cannot reliably enforce retention restrictions, access revocation or correction across connected systems merely because the law identifies it as accountable. Conversely, central agencies should not be able to externalize downstream privacy responsibilities to an under-resourced village whose discretion is confined to data entry. This is a reviewer-derived implication of the institutional architecture described, not a claim that the authors endorse such allocation.

The paper's recommendation of harmonized rules, training, security investment and participatory oversight is proportionate to its doctrinal purpose. A fair counterargument is that a legal analysis need not prescribe a detailed responsibility matrix or technical architecture. That defense holds against demanding implementation evidence from the authors. It does not resolve the stronger promise that the proposed construction can provide *operational guidance*: it remains unclear who authorizes secondary uses, documents intergovernmental transfers, decides a correction request when systems disagree, or funds and supervises compliance controls. Those decisions are precisely where the difference between legal responsibility and effective governing capacity becomes visible.

There is a related tension in the remedies discussion. The article identifies complaint handling, breach notification, impact assessment and litigation as potential obligations or protections, while also describing limited local legal literacy and weak oversight. However, enumerating statutory remedies does not establish that villagers can invoke them without jeopardizing their access to services or encountering the same officials whose conduct they are challenging. The institutional question is which independent, accessible channel receives objections, preserves evidence and ensures that corrective decisions propagate to recipients of shared data. Without such a channel, rights may be formally available but practically dependent on the discretion they are meant to constrain.

The review archive's assessment of Indonesia's Digital Public Service Mall examines how central coordination integrates decentralized services without necessarily establishing equitable outcomes. Pirdaus and colleagues address a complementary problem inside that coordination: the limits of processing authority when local public services become data-intensive. The archive's review of privacy and surveillance in Tanzania similarly distinguishes statutory protections from operative oversight, but the present article concerns routine administrative processing rather than surveillance authorization. Its contribution is a jurisdiction-specific legal synthesis, not an empirically validated governance model or a new technical control.

The appropriate editorial disposition is to accept the authors' central legal constraint while qualifying their implementation claims. A practical extension would map three village workflows, such as domicile certification, social assistance eligibility and transfer to a national population system, to the precise lawful purpose, deciding authority, data recipient, retention rule, correction path and independent complaint route. Such a mapping could reveal whether formal controller responsibility corresponds to the power to enforce the PDP Law. Until then, *conditional authority* is a credible doctrinal boundary, not yet a demonstrated operating arrangement for decentralized data governance.

## Key Insight

Village authority can justify collecting data for local public services, but accountability under Indonesia's PDP Law remains incomplete until each processing purpose, intergovernmental transfer, and citizen remedy has an identifiable decision-maker and enforceable control.
