# Paper Review

## Review

Kenneth Oduma Chiabuotu Odanwu and Anokwuru Christian Uche distinguish functional autonomy from moral autonomy. Sections 5 and 7 argue that independent planning, adaptation and goal pursuit do not establish conscious self-legislation or moral responsibility. The institutional consequence is that delegating execution to an artificial agent does not discharge the responsibilities of its designers, operators or deploying institutions. This is a philosophical argument, developed through conceptual clarification and comparative reading rather than empirical investigation. Its sectoral examples illustrate possible consequences without establishing their prevalence or magnitude.

The distinction between negative and positive freedom in section 2 gives the analysis a more consequential application than the question of machine personhood alone. An agent can expand a person's practical capabilities while restricting the conditions under which that person forms or exercises choices. Section 6 identifies automation bias, manipulation and surveillance alongside productivity gains. These effects can coexist: a person may accomplish more through an automated intermediary while acquiring less discretion over the information and alternatives available to them. The relevant governance question is therefore not simply how much work the machine performs, but who controls the conditions of human choice.

The philosophical conclusion is less settled than the authors suggest. Sections 3 and 7 acknowledge compatibilism and functionalist accounts of mind, yet the argument from computational dependence to the absence of genuine autonomy does not independently resolve those positions. Dependence on prior causes is not sufficient to defeat compatibilist agency; nor does the absence of established evidence of machine consciousness prove its impossibility. The narrower conclusion, that current functional performance provides no sufficient basis for transferring moral responsibility to machines, survives these objections. It does not require settling the metaphysics of consciousness.

The more material governance limitation arises in section 8, where the authors recommend that humans retain authority to review, challenge and override consequential decisions. This is a defensible normative requirement, not evidence that such authority remains exercisable. An institution can retain responsibility while depending on a vendor for execution records, intervention interfaces or the ability to suspend an ongoing task. An affected person can possess moral agency while having no effective means to challenge an automated determination. Conversely, an infrastructure operator may exercise substantial control without owing the same duties as the deploying institution. These are institutional implications of the paper's framing, not findings from its illustrative examples.

A fair response is that a philosophical inquiry need not specify authorization protocols or administrative remedies. That objection resolves any demand for a technical implementation as a condition of philosophical validity. It does not resolve the institutional question introduced by the recommendation for meaningful oversight: which humans possess the effective capacity to intervene, and whose freedom is protected by that arrangement? Assigning oversight to a nominally responsible actor without addressing its dependence on the execution infrastructure risks preserving responsibility while relocating control.

The archive's review of *AI Agents Push Humans Out of the Loop* already examines how oversight capacity can deteriorate through cognitive offloading and approval fatigue. *Authority Continuity Across Agentic Trajectories* separates continued goal pursuit from authority for the next action. *Leveraging Human Rights Frameworks for Agentic AI Governance* considers the distribution of duties and remedies across organizations. The present paper supplies a philosophical rationale for retaining human responsibility, but does not materially extend those operational analyses.

The distinction between functional and moral autonomy should be retained as a normative constraint against treating machine performance as a basis for discharging human responsibility. It should not be treated as an assurance of human freedom. Institutional governance must separately establish who can authorize, interrupt and contest consequential action, and whether the responsible actors can exercise those powers under actual deployment conditions. This conclusion remains relevant even if future philosophical accounts revise the attribution of moral agency to artificial systems.

## Key Insight

Retaining moral responsibility for an artificial agent does not preserve human freedom when control over the conditions under which that agent acts has migrated beyond the responsible person's effective reach.
