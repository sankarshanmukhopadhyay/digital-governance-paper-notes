# Paper Review

## Review

Simon reconstructs Locke's account of natural equality, consent and fiduciary power as a normative test for algorithmic governance. Automated decisions are not intrinsically illegitimate, but accuracy, administrative consistency and formal compliance do not independently justify public power. Because delegated authority remains held in trust for those subject to it, its exercise must be intelligible, attributable and contestable. Section 4.1 recognizes that computation can restrain inconsistent human discretion; Section 5 insists that consequential decisions still require publicly defensible reasons and identifiable institutional responsibility.

The method is interpretive political philosophy rather than empirical evaluation. Sections 2–3 link rational equality to conditional consent and retained political judgment; Sections 4–5 transpose these premises into three conditions of algorithmic legitimacy: intelligibility, fiduciary accountability and contestability. The article carefully distinguishes ordinary administrative appeal from Locke's extraordinary collective remedy of withdrawing political trust (Section 5.3). Individual redress does not by itself establish a government's authorization, while collective political authority cannot replace the daily means of challenging decisions.

The useful institutional contribution is to resist transferring responsibility from public authorities to technical intermediaries. Choices concerning data, objectives, thresholds, deployment and reliance on outputs remain accountable institutional choices, even where internal model operations are difficult to interpret. The author also notes that ostensibly participatory remedies can reproduce asymmetry: public data trusts can blur responsibility, expert audits can consolidate technical gatekeeping, and sandboxes can privilege powerful participants unless their mandates and review conditions are constrained. The analysis thus does not mistake disclosure or stakeholder participation for sufficient legitimacy.

One material tension concerns the degree of intelligibility required. Parts of Sections 2–3 imply that opaque operations dissolve legitimate authority, while Section 4.1 allows that algorithmic systems can provide more consistent and auditable administration. These claims can coexist if public intelligibility attaches primarily to reasons for adopting the system, substantive criteria and evidence supporting a decision, and independent corrective review, rather than requiring all citizens to reconstruct model internals. The article distinguishes intelligibility from code disclosure but does not precisely establish this threshold. Without that boundary, technical opacity may be treated as constitutional invalidity even where an accountable official can justify and reconsider the outcome.

Fiduciary accountability also raises a practical allocation problem. A public body may be legally answerable but lack the contractual access, technical capacity or authority to audit and override vendor-controlled decisions. The paper demands identifiable responsibility but does not specify records duties, review powers or enforceable remedies. Those are properly implementation questions beyond a stated philosophical inquiry, not evidence against its normative reconstruction. They matter when applying the proposed criterion: an official's title cannot substitute for an institution's ability to disclose reasons, revisit judgments and compel correction.

Compared with the archive's *Procedural Fairness, Accountability, and Legitimacy in the AI-Enabled State*, which studies perceptions of legitimacy, Simon examines the justification of authority. Compared with *Accountability in AI and Automated Decision-Making*, she provides a philosophical grounding for independent administrative review rather than procurement and remedy design. A useful application would assess one consequential public decision against its lawful mandate, public reasons, accountable principal, independent evidence access and effective challenge pathway. The paper establishes why these questions belong in a legitimacy assessment, not whether any particular deployed arrangement meets them.

## Key Insight

Algorithmically mediated public authority remains legitimate only when its institutional principals can justify consequential decisions, answer for their exercise, and permit effective contestation; technical consistency is not a substitute for those duties.
