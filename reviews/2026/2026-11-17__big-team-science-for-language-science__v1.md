# Paper Review

## Review

Faytak and colleagues examine Big Team Science (BTS) as an institutional method for producing more comparable and more linguistically diverse research. Their central proposition is conditional: pooling researchers, sites and expertise can extend the empirical reach of language science, but the coordination required for comparability can undermine the contextual flexibility needed to include understudied languages and differently resourced research sites. The paper is a methodological review and proposal, not an evaluation showing that BTS automatically produces representative samples or more legitimate knowledge. Its governance relevance follows from the organization of research itself: whoever defines the common protocol also shapes which questions can be tested, which sites can participate and which observations become admissible evidence.

Sections 2 and 5 distinguish three research purposes: confirmatory experimentation, exploratory observation and dataset creation. The distinction prevents an inappropriate universal prescription. A registered, pre-specified experimental design may reduce researcher degrees of freedom and improve replication, but it is often poorly suited to languages, participants or collection environments that require substantial adaptation. Exploratory work can admit variation and generate hypotheses without claiming the same cross-site comparability. The authors correctly reject the assumption that a large team replaces smaller, situated research. Their own conclusion acknowledges that maximizing typological breadth and maximizing methodological uniformity can be competing objectives, not simultaneous gains guaranteed by scale.

The paper makes this practical through SPAML, GramBank and ManySpeechAnalyses. SPAML illustrates how a small leadership group can define an experimental protocol and analysis plan before recruiting collaborators. The paper notes that researchers who joined later to translate materials or collect data received authorship but did not influence the protocol or planned analyses (Section 2.1). GramBank demonstrates coordinated coding rules and quality checks across a much wider linguistic dataset; ManySpeechAnalyses demonstrates that analysts examining the same speech data can arrive at categorically different interpretations (Sections 2.2, 2.3 and 4). These cases identify three distinct forms of control: design authority, inclusion and coding authority, and interpretive authority. They are not interchangeable, and increasing the number of credited contributors need not distribute any of them.

The authors do not idealize central coordination. Section 3.1.2 explicitly recognizes abuses of leadership power, suppressed criticism, role ambiguity, disproportionate credit and fears of exclusion. More significantly, a footnote acknowledges that their own recruitment efforts failed to achieve much institutional and geographic diversity: all but three contributors were affiliated with institutions in countries classified as high-income. That disclosure is not a proof that BTS is structurally exclusionary. It is evidence that an inclusive invitation and a large author list do not themselves overcome network effects and unequal incentives. The authors further identify institutional practices that devalue consortium authorship, restrict cross-border funding and impose uneven research ethics and data-sharing requirements. Those are not incidental administrative frictions. They determine who can afford to participate and whose participation counts professionally.

The methodological apparatus is concrete. Sections 5 and 6 address site qualification, equipment calibration, culturally appropriate stimuli, translated consent instruments, metadata harmonization, quality thresholds, multilevel analysis and data stewardship. In particular, the discussion of locally meaningful social categories and dialect labels shows why schema conformity cannot be equated with semantic equivalence. Centrally validated metadata may improve processing while concealing locally material distinctions if mappings are imposed without adequate expertise. The authors instead recommend documenting conditions and preserving local norms through equivalence mappings and ontologies. Their discussion of spoken and signed language, local literacy, and the limitations of supposedly neutral laboratory environments further demonstrates that rigorous measurement depends on the validity of the research setting, not simply on identical equipment or procedures.

The consequential institutional gap is narrower than a demand for an exhaustive governance framework. The paper already recommends community consultation, local language expertise, transparent contribution requirements, consensus practices, outside stakeholder input and culturally appropriate data management. A fair response to governance criticism is that a methodological review need not legislate a single decision procedure for every research consortium. That defense holds. Yet the argument that BTS can diversify scientific participation depends, in part, on whether those consulted can influence consequential choices rather than merely comply with them. The paper supplies examples of consultation and centrally managed quality control but no general method for distinguishing local adaptations that should be accepted, negotiated or rejected, nor a clear account of who settles disputes when rigor, local relevance and community interests conflict. This is a deployment dependency of the proposed research model, not an omission that invalidates the methodological synthesis.

A further distinction concerns data access and stewardship. Section 6.3 recognizes jurisdictional restrictions, identifiability of audio and video data, the possibility that anonymity is culturally inappropriate, and the need to specify reuse terms. These are substantive safeguards. Nonetheless, standardized licensing and technically reproducible provenance do not settle whether researchers or language communities retain meaningful influence over secondary uses of culturally situated material. The concern is not that the authors claim otherwise. It is that a central archive can preserve evidentiary integrity while consolidating practical discretion over downstream access and interpretation. Any operational BTS consortium seeking both comparability and inclusion should identify who approves exceptional data handling, who can request correction or restriction, and how those decisions remain visible to collaborators.

In the existing archive, the review of *How Can AI Support Language Digitization and Digital Inclusion?* examines community authority over linguistic data and technical standards. Faytak and colleagues address a different, logically prior layer: how collaborative research institutions select and standardize the evidence from which later linguistic resources and generalizations are built. This is an extension in institutional focus, not a newly invented mechanism or a demonstrated governance outcome. The paper also complements the archive's analysis of LLM-driven scientific productivity, which concerns output incentives rather than distributed research protocol design.

The appropriate conclusion is to adopt BTS as a complementary method with explicitly bounded claims. The paper establishes why distributed coordination can increase evidentiary scope and why uniformity has costs; it does not establish that scale produces epistemic representation. For practical adoption, a project charter should identify leadership decision rights, site inclusion criteria, permitted protocol deviations, escalation routes for contested exclusions, authorship rules and data reuse decisions. Assessing whether these arrangements change actual participation in study design, rather than only recruitment counts, would materially test the institutional promise. Scientific comparability is a valid objective, but the authority to define comparability must itself be open to scrutiny.

## Key Insight

In cross-linguistic Big Team Science, methodological comparability depends on central coordination, but epistemic inclusion depends on whether local researchers can influence the standards that determine which languages, data and interpretations count.
