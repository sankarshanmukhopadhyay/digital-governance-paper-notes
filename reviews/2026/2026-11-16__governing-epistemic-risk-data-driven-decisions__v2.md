# Paper Review

## Review

Kilwake and Ongare's Epistemic Risk Governance Framework (ERGF) unifies data quality, platform control, algorithmic governance and information integrity across the data-to-decision chain. The useful distinction is not that governance must cover more layers. It is that the power to decide which claims are collected, amplified, checked and treated as credible can shape an institutional decision even when the final model is explainable. Tables 2 and 3 translate that insight into risk families and six diagnostic dimensions. Platform visibility and concentrated verification authority become objects of scrutiny alongside data quality and model opacity.

This synthesis is a plausible diagnostic vocabulary rather than a validated assurance framework. The authors describe an integrative literature review and concept-matrix coding, but Section 2.2 retains the instruction to insert screening counts. The matrix is not supplied, and the published funding declaration retains a placeholder. These publication-state defects do not refute the conceptual distinctions. They prevent independent reconstruction of how the thirteen risks were selected and merged, which matters when completeness is among the framework's claims.

The decisive argument is Proposition P7: effective downstream governance is constrained by upstream data or platform weaknesses, which the paper calls non-compensatory. The claim has a well-defined domain of plausibility. If critical evidence was never collected or its origin has been irretrievably lost, later explanation may faithfully describe a decision without establishing its factual basis. But that does not prove a general weakest-link rule. Independent sampling, records outside a dominant platform, testimony from excluded groups and adversarial review may restore evidence omitted earlier. The appropriate distinction is between unrecoverable epistemic loss and weaknesses that subsequent controls can detect or correct. Table 4 supplies proposed relationships, not tested measures or a demonstration that compensation is impossible.

The standards comparison deserves a separate judgment. Table 5 marks ERGF as substantially covering all six dimensions while marking NIST, ISO/IEC 42001, the EU AI Act, UNESCO and Kenyan instruments as partial or absent in several cells. The paper expressly calls these assignments indicative. Without section-level coding rules, comparable definitions of coverage and independent scoring, the table records how the authors positioned their taxonomy. It cannot establish that other instruments lack equivalent requirements or that ERGF performs better. The appropriate interpretation is complementarity at the level of questions to ask, not proven regulatory superiority.

The Kenyan examples reinforce that limit. Lending, identity, platform work and electoral information are contexts in which the dimensions are relevant, but their treatment is illustrative. They do not report implementations showing that a completed checklist improves outcomes. The framework is therefore most credible when used to structure an audit interview or research design, with explicit recognition that the presence of controls is not evidence that the underlying knowledge is reliable.

One institutional distinction remains material. D3 asks who governs visibility; D4 asks who governs fact-checkers; D5 asks whether people can contest outputs. None of those questions identifies a competent authority to compel disclosure, adjudicate disputed evidence or order a remedy. This is not a missing engineering feature the conceptual authors promised. It determines whether using ERGF as a governance assurance label would be justified. An institution that checks every dimension while retaining control over the rules, evidence and review process could still leave affected people without effective contestation.

Compared with the archive's *Governance Laundering* review, ERGF interrogates the upstream formation and circulation of claims rather than the replayability of downstream authorization evidence. That is a genuine difference in analytical object. The archive should retain ERGF as a domain-spanning diagnostic taxonomy, but not accept P7 as a universal law or Table 5 as proof of superior coverage. Reproducible coding and observations distinguishing recoverable from irrecoverable upstream defects would be needed to elevate those claims.

## Key Insight

ERGF adds a useful map of where information becomes authoritative, but its asserted weakest-link rule and self-scored standards superiority remain hypotheses; the framework should guide questions, not certify epistemic reliability.
