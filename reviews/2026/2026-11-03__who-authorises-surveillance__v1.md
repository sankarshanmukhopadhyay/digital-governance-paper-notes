# Paper Review

## Review

Bayra and Bulut argue that the decisive constitutional question in coercive digital surveillance precedes privacy balancing: who is entitled to authorise interference, and which institutions can constrain and review that authority? Their proposed test combines four cumulative conditions: a legislative reserve over fundamental choices, independent prior authorisation, effective ex post oversight and remedies, and public accountability when functions pass to private actors. The conditions become more demanding across four intervention levels, from targeted metadata access to device intrusion and real-time biometric identification. Passing this structural test does not displace necessity and proportionality; it establishes the authority within which those assessments can legitimately operate.

The comparative doctrinal analysis draws on European Court of Human Rights and Court of Justice of the EU decisions, the European Parliament's Pegasus inquiry, legislation and European Commission reports. Poland and Hungary illustrate how weakened judicial independence can coincide with executive surveillance expansion. Ukraine tests the framework under EU accession pressure and martial law, while the United Kingdom's ministerial and judicial-commissioner 'double lock' shows that strengthening prior approval alone does not settle downstream oversight. These are purposively selected legal-institutional illustrations, not independent empirical measurements of whether each oversight system works in practice.

The contribution is an integrated account of *authority allocation*, rather than a newly discovered constitutional principle. Existing privacy, separation-of-powers, automation and private-delegation literatures are brought into one diagnostic architecture. The paper usefully insists that a technically enforceable order, or a proportionate intervention, cannot cure a defective public mandate. It also identifies a second point of institutional migration: surveillance thresholds, targeting logic and practical access choices can move from legislatures and courts into administrative configuration and supplier-controlled systems. Procurement and contract compliance are consequently insufficient substitutes for public-law accountability.

The framework's cumulative logic is convincing as a normative proposition, but its tiers are less settled as operational standards. Grouping bulk analysis, device infiltration and real-time biometrics by intensity simplifies distinct dimensions of intrusiveness: persistence, population scale, inferential reach, purpose expansion and reversibility. Repeated metadata collection can expose a person's associations more comprehensively than a narrowly bounded content interception. A risk-sensitive authorisation process would therefore need explicit classification factors, escalation rules and evidence supporting the classification, rather than relying on the nominal technology category.

A related implementation gap concerns the independence and efficacy of oversight. The paper specifies who should approve and review, but not how institutional independence would be demonstrated under political pressure, how secret orders could be made contestable without compromising legitimate investigations, or how operators would preserve evidence, suspend unauthorised processing, notify affected people and deliver remedies. Its discussion of private providers correctly preserves public accountability, yet leaves open how audit access, contractual duties, legal compulsion and sanctions would operate across borders. Those are requirements for an operational regime, not proof that the doctrinal proposal fails.

A fair counterargument is that the authors explicitly present a structural-normative diagnostic rather than an implementation specification or field study. Their acknowledgement of substantive prohibitions, emergency procedures and the limited evidence on Ukraine appropriately bounds the claim. The remaining question is whether the framework can distinguish formal compliance from effective restraint. A practical evaluation should test actual warrant records against legislative mandates, verify reviewer independence, measure notification and appeal accessibility, and examine supplier accountability under disputed, emergency and revoked authorisations.

The paper therefore adds a useful constitutional prerequisite to digital-governance assessment: traceability can show that an order was issued, and compliance can show that a system executed it as specified, but neither establishes the legitimacy of the ordering institution. Its enduring implication is that governance of coercive infrastructure must preserve an independently contestable chain of public authority from legal mandate through execution to remedy. Without that chain, increasingly sophisticated surveillance controls may only make unauthorised power more reliably executable.

## Key Insight

A surveillance measure cannot acquire legitimacy through proportionality or technical compliance when the authority to order it, the independence of approval, or the ability to contest it is defective.
